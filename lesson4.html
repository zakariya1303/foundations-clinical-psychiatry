Capacity & Safeguarding

A practical framework for assessing capacity and recognising safeguarding concerns in psychiatric practice.

Introduction

Capacity and safeguarding are central to safe psychiatric practice. They are particularly important because psychiatric illness can affect decision-making, vulnerability, risk, relationships, and a patient’s ability to protect themselves from harm.

This lesson focuses on two linked but distinct areas. Capacity asks whether a patient can make a specific decision at a specific time. Safeguarding asks whether a patient, child, or vulnerable adult may be at risk of abuse, neglect, exploitation, coercion, or harm.

Capacity Assessment

Capacity should always be presumed unless there is evidence to suggest otherwise. A patient is not assumed to lack capacity simply because they have a mental illness, make an unwise decision, or disagree with medical advice.

Capacity is decision-specific and time-specific. This means a patient may have capacity to make one decision but not another, and their capacity may change depending on their mental state, physical health, intoxication, distress, or level of confusion.

A practical capacity assessment considers whether the patient can understand the relevant information, retain it long enough to make the decision, weigh up the information, and communicate their choice.

A useful way to explore this is to ask the patient to explain the decision in their own words:

“Can you tell me what you understand about the situation?”
“What do you think might happen if you accept or refuse this option?”
“Can you explain why you have come to that decision?”

Optimising Capacity

Before deciding that someone lacks capacity, clinicians should try to support the patient to make the decision where possible. This may involve using simpler language, repeating information, offering written explanations, using interpreters, treating pain, allowing time for distress to settle, or reassessing later if capacity may fluctuate.

This is especially relevant in psychiatry, where anxiety, psychosis, depression, mania, intoxication, delirium, or medication effects may influence how information is understood or weighed.

Common Pitfalls

A common mistake is assuming that an unwise decision means the patient lacks capacity. Patients are allowed to make decisions that clinicians disagree with, provided they can understand, retain, weigh up, and communicate the relevant information.

Another common pitfall is treating capacity as global. Capacity is not a general label. It must relate to a specific decision at a specific time, such as consenting to medication, agreeing to admission, refusing blood tests, or deciding where to live.

Mental Capacity Act vs Mental Health Act

The Mental Capacity Act and Mental Health Act are often confused, but they serve different purposes.

  • Mental Capacity Act: relates to decision-making capacity. It is used when a person may lack capacity to make a specific decision, and decisions must then be made in their best interests.
  • Mental Health Act: relates to the detention and treatment of mental disorder when specific legal criteria are met, usually involving risk to the patient or others and the need for assessment or treatment.

Importantly, a patient detained under the Mental Health Act may still have capacity for many decisions. Equally, a patient who lacks capacity is not automatically detained under the Mental Health Act.

Safeguarding

Safeguarding involves recognising and responding to situations where a patient, child, or vulnerable adult may be at risk of harm. In psychiatry, safeguarding is particularly important because mental illness can increase vulnerability to abuse, neglect, exploitation, coercive control, financial abuse, domestic violence, self-neglect, and unsafe environments.

Safeguarding questions should be asked sensitively and ideally in a private setting. Patients may not disclose concerns immediately, particularly if they feel ashamed, frightened, dependent on the perpetrator, or worried about consequences.

Helpful questions include:

“Do you feel safe at home?”
“Has anyone made you feel frightened, controlled, or unsafe?”
“Is anyone hurting you or stopping you from getting help?”

Children and Dependents

Psychiatric assessment should also consider whether there are children, dependents, or vulnerable adults who may be affected by the patient’s presentation.

For example, severe depression, psychosis, substance misuse, domestic abuse, or suicidal ideation may raise concerns about parenting capacity, supervision, household safety, or emotional wellbeing of children in the home.

This does not mean making assumptions or blaming the patient. It means thinking broadly about safety and ensuring the right support is involved.

Escalation

If safeguarding concerns are identified, they should be escalated to a senior clinician and the appropriate safeguarding team. In urgent situations, immediate action may be required to protect the patient or others.

Good safeguarding practice involves listening carefully, documenting clearly, sharing concerns appropriately, and balancing confidentiality with the duty to protect people from serious harm.

Clinical Pearls

Capacity is presumed unless proven otherwise and must always be decision-specific and time-specific. An unwise decision does not automatically mean a patient lacks capacity. Safeguarding should remain in the background of every psychiatric assessment, especially where there are concerns about vulnerability, coercion, self-neglect, domestic abuse, children, or dependent adults. If in doubt, escalate early and involve senior support.
