Mental State Examination

A practical framework for assessing observed mental state throughout the consultation.

Introduction

The Mental State Examination, often shortened to MSE, acts as the psychiatric equivalent of a physical examination.

While Lesson 1 focused on gathering the psychiatric history, the MSE focuses on what you observe and assess during the interaction itself.

The acronym ASEPTIC is often used to help students structure the MSE: Appearance & behaviour, Speech, Emotion (mood/affect), Perception, Thought, Insight, and Cognition.

The MSE begins the moment the patient enters the room.

Appearance and Behaviour

Appearance and behaviour can provide significant diagnostic clues before the patient even begins speaking.

Observation within the MSE includes clothing, hygiene, eye contact, facial expression, body language, psychomotor activity, rapport, and behaviour throughout the consultation.

Bright flamboyant clothing, pacing, overfamiliarity, and exaggerated confidence may suggest mania, whereas poor self-care, reduced eye contact, slowed movement, and withdrawal may suggest depression.

Agitation may indicate anxiety, psychosis, delirium, intoxication, or mania.

Speech

Speech assessment includes rate, volume, tone, quantity, and fluency.

Rapid pressured speech may suggest mania, while quiet slowed speech may occur in severe depression. Loud intense speech may reflect agitation or intoxication, whereas hesitant speech may suggest anxiety, psychosis, cognitive impairment, or emotional distress.

Example: “Speech was normal in rate, tone, and volume.”

Mood and Affect

Mood refers to the patient’s sustained emotional state and is usually subjective, meaning it is primarily described by the patient.

Example: “How would you describe your mood recently?”

Affect refers to the emotional expression observed throughout the consultation. Affect may appear flat, reactive, blunted, labile, congruent, or incongruent.

Importantly, mood and affect do not always match. A patient may describe feeling “fine” while appearing visibly tearful or withdrawn.

Thought Form

Thought form refers to how thoughts are organised and expressed. In psychiatry, how a patient speaks can sometimes be just as important as what they are saying.

Thoughts may appear logical and goal-directed, or alternatively become tangential, circumstantial, disorganised, or rapidly shifting between topics.

Flight of ideas may suggest mania, whereas loosening of associations may suggest psychosis. The way a patient communicates can therefore provide major insight into underlying pathology.

Thought Content

Thought content explores the beliefs and themes occupying the patient’s mind. Thought content may include delusions, paranoia, guilt, hopelessness, obsessions, or suicidal thoughts.

If potentially delusional beliefs are identified, it is often better to gently explore them rather than aggressively challenge them.

Examples:
“Is there any other possible explanation for this?”
“How would you feel if someone suggested this may not be the case?”

These questions can help assess insight while maintaining rapport.

Perception

Perceptual assessment mainly explores hallucinations and perceptual disturbances. Questions around hallucinations should usually be phrased sensitively and normalised where possible.

Example: “Sometimes when people are under significant stress, they may hear or see things that others cannot. Has anything like this happened to you?”

Visual hallucinations may suggest underlying organic pathology and should always raise concern for delirium, neurological disease, intoxication, or dementia.

Cognition

Cognition assessment explores orientation, memory, attention, and concentration. Basic cognition assessment may include asking the patient their name, location, current date, and understanding of the current situation.

Attention can be assessed using tasks such as months backwards or serial sevens.

Where cognitive impairment is suspected, more formal assessments such as the Mini Mental State Examination or Montreal Cognitive Assessment may provide further insight into executive function, language, memory, and attention.

Insight

Insight assesses the patient’s awareness and understanding of their condition.

Explore whether the patient:

  • Recognises symptoms
  • Accepts illness
  • Understands consequences
  • Is willing to engage with treatment

Insight is particularly important because it often reflects overall risk, treatment engagement, decision-making, need for admission, and overall safety.

Collateral history from family, carers, or close friends may provide valuable additional insight into behavioural change or deterioration.

Presenting the MSE

A concise MSE presentation may sound like:

“The patient appeared well kempt and cooperative. Speech was pressured and rapid. Mood was subjectively elevated with reactive affect. Thought form demonstrated flight of ideas. Thought content included grandiose beliefs. No hallucinations were elicited. Cognition appeared grossly intact. Insight was limited.”

Clinical Pearls

The MSE is heavily observational and begins from first contact. Clothing, body language, eye contact, speech, and behaviour can all provide major clues before direct questioning even begins.

Insight remains one of the most important parts of the MSE because it often reflects illness severity, overall risk, and engagement with treatment.
