Risk Assessment

A structured approach to assessing suicide/self-harm risk, risk to others, vulnerability, and protective factors.

Introduction

Risk assessment is one of the most important aspects of psychiatric assessment.

The aim is not to predict the future perfectly, but rather to understand current concerns, identify escalating risk, appreciate protective factors, and determine the safest management plan.

Good psychiatric risk assessment requires structure, empathy, observation, and communication.

In real clinical practice, risk assessment is paramount because patient and public safety always remain central priorities within psychiatry.

Within an OSCE or ISCE setting, strong risk assessment is also one of the clearest ways of demonstrating safe clinical practice to the examiner.

Suicide Risk and Self-Harm

Risk assessment should explore suicidal thoughts, intent, planning, access to means, previous attempts, self-harm history, and protective factors.

Questions should remain calm, compassionate, and non-judgemental. Many patients may feel embarrassed, frightened, or offended by direct questioning around suicide. A softer normalising approach is therefore often preferred.

Example: “When people feel very overwhelmed or hopeless, they may sometimes experience thoughts of harming themselves or others. Has this ever happened to you?”

Further exploration may include:

“Have you thought about how you might harm yourself?”
“Have you ever acted on these thoughts previously?”
“What has stopped you from acting on them?”

Importantly, asking about suicide does not increase suicidal thoughts and often helps patients feel understood.

Risk to Others

Assessment should also explore aggression, violence, paranoia, command hallucinations, and risk to others.

This becomes particularly important in psychosis, severe agitation, intoxication, and certain personality disorders.

Vulnerability and Safeguarding

It is important to assess whether the patient may be vulnerable due to abuse, neglect, exploitation, domestic violence, financial coercion, or cognitive impairment.

Example: “Do you feel safe at home?”

Safeguarding concerns may require escalation.

Static and Dynamic Risk Factors

Static risk factors are historical long-term factors that are unlikely to change significantly, such as previous suicide attempts, chronic mental illness, or childhood trauma.

Dynamic risk factors fluctuate over time and may indicate worsening risk. Examples include acute stressors, intoxication, worsening mental state, medication non-compliance, relationship breakdown, or current suicidal ideation.

Understanding both static and dynamic risk factors helps build a fuller picture of overall patient safety.

Protective Factors

Protective factors may significantly reduce overall risk. Protective factors may include supportive relationships, religious beliefs, children or dependents, employment, future goals, and engagement with healthcare services.

Helpful question: “What has helped you get through difficult periods previously?”

Overdose Risk Assessment

A useful structure for overdose histories follows a simple before, during, and after framework.

Before: Explore emotional state, triggers, and circumstances leading to the overdose.

During: Clarify the substance taken, quantity, timing, alcohol involvement, co-ingestion, and overall intent.

After: Assess regret, help-seeking behaviour, current suicidal thoughts, and ongoing intent.

It may also be important to explore whether the patient left a note, attempted to avoid being found, or took steps suggesting high suicidal intent.

Patients may describe feeling relieved to have survived, deeply guilty about the attempt itself, or disappointed that the overdose did not work. Exploring these emotions carefully can provide valuable insight into ongoing risk.

De-escalation and Communication

Patients discussing risk may feel frightened, ashamed, emotionally overwhelmed, or fearful of being judged. Communication style therefore matters enormously.

A calm tone, active listening, validation of distress, and non-judgemental language can significantly improve rapport and often leads to more honest disclosure.

Where significant risk is identified, it is important to involve senior colleagues and the wider multidisciplinary team early.

Clinical Pearls

Risk assessment should remain continuous throughout the consultation rather than feeling like a separate interrogation at the end.

Previous suicide attempts remain one of the strongest risk factors for future self-harm, while protective factors remain equally important when assessing overall safety.

Careful documentation and collateral history may both become extremely important clinically and medico-legally.
