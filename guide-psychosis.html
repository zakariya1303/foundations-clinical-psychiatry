Psychosis

A sensitive, structured approach to assessing psychotic symptoms, risk, and possible organic causes.

Overview

Psychosis involves a loss of contact with reality and may present with hallucinations, delusions, disorganised thinking, paranoia, or behavioural disturbance. Causes may be psychiatric, substance-related, neurological, or secondary to organic pathology.

A key challenge in psychiatry is balancing effective assessment with compassionate communication. Patients experiencing psychosis may already feel frightened, suspicious, overwhelmed, or misunderstood, meaning the way questions are phrased is extremely important.

Psychosis may develop gradually, with subtle behavioural or functional decline preceding more obvious hallucinations or delusions.

Key Symptoms

  • Auditory hallucinations
  • Visual hallucinations
  • Delusions
  • Paranoia
  • Thought disorder
  • Disorganised behaviour
  • Reduced insight
  • Functional decline
  • Social withdrawal

Important Questions

Rather than asking abrupt or highly direct questions, psychiatric assessment often uses softer, normalising language to reduce distress and encourage openness.

For example:

“Sometimes when people are under significant stress or going through difficult experiences, they may hear or see things that other people cannot. Has anything like this ever happened to you?”
“Some people may feel as though others are watching them or talking about them. Have you ever experienced anything similar?”

These approaches are important because they:

  • Reduce stigma and embarrassment
  • Avoid making the patient feel judged
  • Encourage openness and trust
  • Allow clinicians to gather accurate information sensitively

There is often a fine balance between obtaining clinically important information while maintaining rapport and ensuring the patient feels safe and respected throughout the assessment.

Additional questioning should explore:

  • Risk to self or others
  • Command hallucinations
  • Substance misuse
  • Functional decline
  • Insight
  • Organic symptoms
  • Sleep disturbance
  • Collateral history from family or friends

Initial Management

  • Assess immediate risk to self and others
  • Exclude delirium and organic pathology
  • Full physical examination and bloods
  • Substance misuse assessment
  • Early psychiatric involvement
  • Antipsychotic medication under specialist guidance
  • Consider admission where there is severe agitation, behavioural disturbance, or inability to care for self

Organic causes should always be considered, particularly in:

  • Older patients
  • Acute onset presentations
  • Visual hallucinations
  • Associated neurological symptoms
  • Fluctuating cognition

Clinical Pearls

  • Avoid directly reinforcing delusions
  • Validate emotional distress without confirming false beliefs
  • Visual hallucinations may suggest organic causes
  • Functional decline is often an important early clue
  • Collateral history is frequently invaluable
  • Psychosis may coexist with mood disorders
