Psychiatric History Taking

A structured framework for taking a psychiatric history with empathy, observation, and clear communication.

Introduction

Psychiatric history taking aims to understand not only a patient’s symptoms, but also their emotions, behaviours, coping strategies, relationships, and wider social circumstances. Unlike many other specialties, psychiatry relies heavily on communication, rapport, observation, and understanding the patient’s lived experience.

This lesson focuses primarily on taking a psychiatric history rather than performing a full Mental State Examination or formal risk assessment. The MSE and psychiatric risk assessment are explored in greater depth in Lessons 2 and 3.

It is also important to remember that nobody is expected to obtain every single detail perfectly in one consultation. Psychiatry histories can become extremely broad, particularly in complex patients. The purpose of this framework is therefore to help guide your thinking and highlight the themes you should ideally try to explore when time allows.

Beginning the Consultation

Introduce yourself clearly and create a calm, non-judgemental environment.

Example: “Hi, my name is Zakariya. I’m a medical student working with the psychiatry team today.”

Confirm the patient’s identity and explain confidentiality.

Example: “Everything we discuss is confidential unless there are concerns about your safety or someone else’s safety.”

Unlike many other specialties, psychiatry also begins with observation from the very first moment. Even before direct questioning, important clues may already be present through:

  • Clothing
  • Eye contact
  • Facial expression
  • Body language
  • Level of agitation
  • Hygiene
  • General demeanour

For example, flamboyant brightly coloured clothing and overfamiliar behaviour may point towards mania, while poor self-care and withdrawn posture may suggest severe depression.

Presenting Complaint

Start openly.

Example: “What has brought you in today?”

Psychiatric presentations are often emotionally loaded, meaning patients may reveal important clues very early in the interaction.

Patients should generally be given time and space to speak freely initially. However, psychiatry also requires flexibility. In conditions such as mania, patients may become extremely talkative, tangential, or difficult to interrupt. In these situations it becomes important to gently and professionally guide the conversation so key information can still be obtained while maintaining rapport.

History of Presenting Complaint

The history of presenting complaint explores the patient’s symptoms in greater depth.

Important areas include:

  • Timing and onset
  • Triggers or stressors
  • Severity
  • Functional impact
  • Associated symptoms
  • Risk
  • Impact on relationships, work, or education

It is also important to explore what may have changed around the onset of symptoms. Recent bereavement, social isolation, relationship breakdown, chronic stress, financial difficulties, or worsening physical health may all contribute.

Psychiatric symptoms may also overlap with physical illness. Conditions such as thyroid disease, fibromyalgia, chronic fatigue syndrome, neurological disease, chronic pain conditions, endocrine disorders, or anaemia can all significantly affect mood, energy, concentration, and behaviour.

Psychiatric History

Previous psychiatric history can provide major clues regarding relapse patterns, illness severity, treatment response, and long-term risk.

It is therefore important to explore any previous diagnoses, psychiatric admissions, community mental health involvement, therapy or counselling, episodes of self-harm, suicide attempts, and previous psychiatric medication use. Understanding what treatments have or have not worked previously can help guide future management and also provides insight into the chronicity and severity of illness.

Medical History

A strong medical history is extremely important in psychiatry. Physical illness may mimic psychiatric disease or significantly worsen mental health symptoms.

Thyroid disease, neurological disorders, epilepsy, chronic pain syndromes, fibromyalgia, chronic fatigue syndrome, endocrine disorders, autoimmune disease, dementia, and delirium may all present with psychiatric-type symptoms such as low mood, fatigue, cognitive impairment, emotional dysregulation, hallucinations, or behavioural change.

Certain infections including syphilis and HIV may also present with neuropsychiatric symptoms and should remain part of the wider differential diagnosis where clinically relevant.

Obstetric and gynaecological history may also become important, particularly in postpartum psychiatric illness, menstrual-related mood disturbance, fertility-related stress, or medication safety during pregnancy.

Drug History

Drug history should include prescribed medication, over-the-counter medication, herbal remedies, recreational drugs, and medication compliance.

Drug history is highly important because both prescribed and recreational substances may contribute directly to psychiatric symptoms. For example, steroids may contribute to mood disturbance or psychosis, while dopaminergic medications can contribute to hallucinations and behavioural changes. Recreational substances may worsen anxiety, paranoia, psychosis, or mood instability.

Drug interactions are also extremely important, particularly in patients taking antidepressants, antipsychotics, mood stabilisers, methadone, or other centrally acting medications.

Family History

Family history can provide valuable insight into both genetic and environmental risk factors.

It is therefore useful to explore any family history of depression, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, suicide, substance misuse, or neurodegenerative disease. Patterns within families may sometimes help support diagnostic suspicion, particularly in conditions such as bipolar disorder or schizophrenia.

Social History

Social history is often one of the most important parts of psychiatric assessment because psychiatry is so heavily influenced by social context.

It is important to understand the patient’s housing situation, employment, relationships, education, finances, support systems, alcohol use, smoking history, recreational drug use, and any safeguarding concerns.

Understanding a patient’s environment often helps explain both the development and maintenance of psychiatric illness.

Forensic History

Forensic history should always be approached calmly and professionally without judgement.

Where relevant, it may be important to explore violence, aggression, previous arrests, prison history, safeguarding concerns, or risk to others. This information may become particularly important when assessing overall safety and future risk.

Communication Skills

Good psychiatric communication relies heavily on empathy, active listening, validation, and non-judgemental questioning.

Patients discussing sensitive topics may feel frightened, ashamed, embarrassed, or worried about being judged. A calm, compassionate approach therefore often leads to more open and honest disclosure.

Clinical Pearls

Psychiatry histories should feel conversational rather than robotic. Observation begins the moment the patient enters the room, and subtle behavioural clues can often provide major diagnostic insight. Physical illness should always remain part of the differential diagnosis, while collateral history from family or carers may provide valuable information where insight is reduced.
