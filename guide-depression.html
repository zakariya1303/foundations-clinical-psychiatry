Depression

A structured approach to recognising depressive symptoms, asking sensitive questions, and planning initial management.

Overview

Depression is a common mood disorder characterised by persistent low mood and/or loss of interest or enjoyment in previously pleasurable activities. Patients may present psychologically, physically, or socially, and symptoms often affect sleep, appetite, concentration, motivation, relationships, and day-to-day functioning.

Depression is increasingly common worldwide and remains one of the leading causes of disability globally. Many patients may struggle for long periods before seeking help, often presenting with fatigue, poor sleep, reduced concentration, or physical complaints rather than directly describing “depression”.

Depression exists on a spectrum ranging from mild symptoms affecting motivation and enjoyment through to severe depressive illness with psychosis, suicidality, and inability to care for oneself.

Key Symptoms

  • Persistent low mood
  • Anhedonia
  • Reduced energy and fatigue
  • Poor concentration
  • Sleep disturbance
  • Appetite or weight change
  • Feelings of guilt or worthlessness
  • Psychomotor slowing
  • Suicidal ideation
  • Social withdrawal

Important Questions

A useful depression screen often begins by exploring mood, energy, and enjoyment.

“How has your mood been recently?”
“Do you feel more tired or low in energy than usual?”
“Do you still enjoy the things you used to enjoy?”

This forms part of a depression screen focusing on the three core components of depression:

  • Low mood
  • Lethargy and reduced energy
  • Anhedonia (reduced enjoyment or pleasure)

These questions aim to identify the central emotional and functional changes associated with depressive illness before exploring symptoms in greater depth.

After this, it is important to assess how symptoms are affecting the patient’s wider life.

Functional impairment:

“How has this affected your day-to-day life?”
“Are you managing work, university, or daily responsibilities as you normally would?”
“Have you found yourself struggling to complete routine tasks?”

Social withdrawal:

“Have you been spending less time with friends or family recently?”
“Do you find yourself isolating yourself more than usual?”

Guilt and hopelessness:

“Do you ever blame yourself for things or feel like you’re letting people down?”
“Do things ever feel hopeless or difficult to see improving?”

You would also explore:

  • Sleep and appetite changes
  • Weight change
  • Concentration and memory
  • Suicidal ideation
  • Self-harm
  • Psychotic symptoms in severe depression
  • Substance use
  • Protective factors and support systems

Initial Management

Management depends on severity, associated risk, and functional impairment.

Initial management may include:

  • Psychoeducation
  • Lifestyle optimisation and sleep support
  • Guided self-help
  • Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)
  • SSRIs such as sertraline
  • Community mental health involvement where appropriate
  • Risk assessment and safety-netting
  • Urgent psychiatric review in severe suicidality, psychosis, or inability to care for self

Psychoeducation refers to helping the patient understand their condition, symptoms, treatment options, expected recovery, and available support. This is important because many patients may feel confused, isolated, ashamed, or frightened by what they are experiencing.

In severe depression, admission may be required where there is:

  • Significant suicide risk
  • Psychotic depression
  • Severe self-neglect
  • Refusal of food or fluids
  • Inability to function safely

Clinical Pearls

  • Patients may present with physical complaints rather than directly describing low mood
  • Always assess suicide risk sensitively
  • Functional impairment often reflects illness severity
  • Depression and anxiety frequently coexist
