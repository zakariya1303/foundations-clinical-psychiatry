Mania

Key features, behavioural observation, and high-yield questions to assess manic symptoms and associated risk.

Overview

Mania is characterised by elevated or irritable mood, increased energy, impulsivity, reduced need for sleep, and behavioural disturbance. It commonly occurs within bipolar affective disorder and may significantly impair judgement, insight, and decision-making.

Patients may initially appear charismatic, energetic, highly motivated, or unusually productive before symptoms escalate into risky behaviour, psychosis, agitation, or severe functional deterioration.

One particularly important feature in psychiatric assessment is observation. In OSCEs and clinical practice, clues to mania may be visible before the patient even speaks.

For example:

  • Extremely vibrant or flamboyant clothing
  • Excessive jewellery or makeup
  • Overfamiliar behaviour
  • Increased animation or pacing
  • Rapid or pressured speech
  • Distractibility
  • Grand gestures or exaggerated confidence

Psychiatry places major emphasis on behavioural observation, meaning valuable information can often be gathered from the patient’s appearance, interactions, and mannerisms throughout the consultation.

Key Symptoms

  • Elevated mood
  • Irritability
  • Reduced sleep
  • Pressured speech
  • Grandiosity
  • Increased goal-directed activity
  • Risk-taking behaviour
  • Flight of ideas
  • Impulsivity
  • Reduced insight

Important Questions

“Have you needed less sleep recently?”
“Do your thoughts feel faster than usual?”
“Have you been feeling unusually confident or energetic?”
“Have you been spending more money or taking more risks?”
“Have people commented that you seem unusually energetic or different?”
“Have you been hearing or seeing things other people cannot?”

Assessment should also explore:

  • Insight
  • Psychotic symptoms
  • Impulsivity
  • Financial risk
  • Sexual risk-taking
  • Safeguarding concerns
  • Substance misuse
  • Risk to self or others

Collateral history is often extremely useful, as patients experiencing mania may lack insight into behavioural change.

Initial Management

  • Assess behavioural disturbance and risk
  • Evaluate for psychosis
  • Reduce environmental stimulation
  • Early psychiatric review
  • Mood stabilisers and/or antipsychotics under specialist guidance
  • Consider admission if there is severe risk or impaired judgement

Where patients are highly agitated or behaviourally disturbed, de-escalation and maintaining a calm environment are extremely important before considering pharmacological management.

Clinical Pearls

  • Patients often have reduced insight
  • Mania may initially appear as confidence or productivity
  • Reduced need for sleep is highly diagnostically important
  • Observation begins the moment the patient enters the room
  • Mania with psychosis may resemble schizophrenia
  • Behavioural clues are often visible before direct questioning
