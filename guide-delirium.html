Delirium

Recognising delirium as a medical emergency, taking a focused history, and prioritising underlying cause and supportive care.

Overview

Delirium is an acute confusional state caused by underlying medical pathology. It is characterised by fluctuating cognition, impaired attention, altered consciousness, and behavioural disturbance. Delirium is a medical emergency and often indicates significant underlying illness.

Patients may become distressed, agitated, frightened, or aggressive, meaning communication and de-escalation skills are highly important during assessment and management.

Delirium is particularly common in elderly or medically unwell patients and may fluctuate significantly throughout the day.

Key Symptoms

  • Acute confusion
  • Fluctuating cognition
  • Reduced attention
  • Disorientation
  • Altered consciousness
  • Visual hallucinations
  • Agitation or hypoactivity
  • Sleep-wake disturbance

Important Questions

“When did the confusion begin?”
“Has the confusion fluctuated throughout the day?”
“Have there been any recent infections, falls, or medication changes?”
“Are they more confused at night?”

Collateral history is particularly important in delirium assessment, as patients may struggle to provide an accurate history themselves.

Initial Management

  • Identify and treat the underlying cause
  • A–E assessment
  • Bloods, urine dip, ECG, imaging where appropriate
  • Medication review
  • Hydration and nutrition
  • Reorientation and supportive care
  • De-escalation techniques and calm communication
  • Avoid unnecessary sedation
  • Consider delirium screening tools such as the 4AT

Common causes are often remembered using the acronym:

PINCH ME
  • Pain
  • Infection
  • Nutrition
  • Constipation
  • Hydration
  • Medication
  • Environment

Medication should only be considered where there is severe distress or risk. Small doses of haloperidol are commonly used where appropriate, although this should be avoided in Parkinson’s disease or Lewy body dementia, where lorazepam may sometimes be preferred under specialist guidance.

Clinical Pearls

  • Delirium is acute and fluctuating
  • Infection, medications, dehydration, and metabolic disturbance are common causes
  • Visual hallucinations are common
  • Delirium and dementia may coexist
  • Non-pharmacological management is extremely important
  • Always consider delirium in sudden confusion
